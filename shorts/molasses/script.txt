In 1919, a city got flooded. Not with water. With two million gallons of syrup.
You see, a company built a giant tank of molasses in Boston, right next to people's homes. Five stories tall.
But the tank leaked. A lot. Kids would bring cups and scoop up the free candy.
So, did the company fix it? No. They painted the tank brown. So you couldn't see the leaks.
They never even filled it with water to test it.
Then, on a warm January afternoon, it burst.
A brown wave, twenty-five feet tall, rolled down the street at thirty-five miles an hour. It ripped buildings off their foundations and bent a steel train bridge.
But here's the worst part. As the sun went down, the molasses got cold. And thicker. And thicker. Until people who could have crawled out were stuck like flies on tape.
Twenty-one people died.
So, how can syrup trap you like that?
Well, it's called viscosity. The colder molasses gets, the harder it is to move. Wading through it is like wading through wet cement.
